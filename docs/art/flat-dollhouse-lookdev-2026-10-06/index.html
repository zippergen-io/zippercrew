Three cast refinements

Each sheet includes the trio, four Human states—neutral, orchestrating, at laptop, expressive idle—and a silhouette strip. All preserve plum Human, coral maker and teal reviewer, while reducing mechanical detail.

A · Quiet Ensemble

The most restrained option: a lightly drawn, longer-proportioned Human beside broad and narrow artificial companions. Quiet clothing and a notebook make the Human feel approachable and adult.

Why it scales

The agents’ very different outlines remain recognizable after reduction. Large torso color areas do more work than small accessories.

Risk

The Human is still too slender and conventionally illustrated. Hair strands, hands and garment detail are the first things to disappear at room scale. This is the least decisive move away from the current art.

B · Offbeat Ensemble

A more expressive cast: a sweeping hair crest and asymmetrical outfit for the Human, a compact headphone-like maker, and a pointed, poised reviewer. The Human’s leaning, listening and directing poses have the strongest individual performance.

Why it scales

Asymmetric hair, the maker’s broad head and the reviewer’s tapered silhouette provide three clear recognition cues.

Risk

Some sculpted shading and joint detail remain. The long Human and reviewer limbs can become thin at small sizes. Carry the gestures forward rather than all the rendering detail.

C · Little Signals — recommended

The strongest graphic reduction: broad hair lobes, large clothing patches, a compact maker with a wide face, and a diamond-headed reviewer with a calm horizontal brow. The Human’s notebook, uneven cuffs and curious head tilt keep the creative-orchestrator identity.

Why it scales

Head outline, body shape, face panel, prop and role color remain useful even after fine detail disappears. New agents can vary one head feature and one body proportion without needing a new rendering style.

Risk

The leaf-like antennae and smiling agents approach the cute end of the brief. Keep active expressions more focused, avoid permanent smiles and bounce, and use quiet UI typography. Human eye marks and hands need a consistent final model sheet before animation production.

Two rooms in the same illustrated family

Both use shallow front-facing space, quiet architecture, minimal furniture, a compact coffee point and a small shared brief. There is no carpet. The large cast is the focal point.

01 · Window Lane

A continuous café frontage: informal Human perch, maker workstation and reviewer reading nook. One shared foreground lane makes movement and handoffs easy to follow. It has the strongest immediate café atmosphere.

Expansion: three uses one compact frontage; five adds two nooks; eight would require a wider scrollable frontage or a connected annex. Risk: a very long lane can look like a lineup, and long-distance handoffs become harder to follow. Best for a small crew, less convincing as the sole large-crew structure.

02 · Linked Studios — recommended

Shallow adjoining bays with open archways and a continuous walking lane. The Human occupies the social seam between work and review, rather than a teacher’s position in front of a board. Bay boundaries group participants without turning the room into a workflow diagram.

Expansion: three occupies a compact work/social bay and a review nook; five expands the review/reference bay; eight adds a third bay. Keep actors at a readable scale and navigate between bays. Risk: offscreen handoffs need a receiver indicator and an explicit jump-to-participant action. Avoid filling every bay with duplicated desks.

Communication grammar and production constraints

Rounded bubbles with a tail show local observable work and, when available, one concise result line. Paper cards have a sender-colored edge, a type label and an optional correlation marker. Cards represent only actual runtime messages; their motion would never be triggered by raw stdout. The pictured “14 passed” and “#17” are illustrative concept copy, not results from a real run.

The drawings combine example states to explain the grammar. Production must sequence send/receive from runtime events, clear superseded local bubbles, preserve FIFO where applicable, and avoid implying a transfer when none occurred. Route marks are transient inspection cues, not a permanently drawn graph.

Build reusable wall/window bays, workstation back/front layers, coffee and shared-brief objects, and separate character pose atlases. Preserve the current Phaser semantic adapter, activity summarizer, real message model, inspector and Room/Run behavior. These full-scene concept paintings must not become single baked production backgrounds.

Recommended production direction

C · Little Signals + 02 · Linked Studios.

Use C’s shape economy and silhouette system, with the expressive listening and directing poses demonstrated by B. Keep the environment as quiet modular bays. The Human remains an offbeat creative participant, not a manager.

Warmest small-crew setting01 · Window Lane: the clearest café atmosphere and shortest initial handoffs.

Compared with the current Room

The improvement is in visual economy: fewer details compete with expression, role and action. The cast can be recognized through outline and prop before reading a label. A consistent flat pose library should also be easier to author and maintain than detailed rendered-looking sprites.

Remaining checks: C’s agents could become too sweet if every pose smiles; define focused, skeptical, waiting and stopped expressions. Normalize eye marks, hands and edge shading across poses. Keep UI text separate from raster art. Test the selected cast at actual in-app size and in motion before treating these static studies as production-ready assets.

Recommended next step: after selecting this combination, author a small consistent C pose atlas and one Linked Studios bay, then replace art behind the existing semantic renderer. Verify the three-person real workflow first; use a renderer-only eight-actor stress test for legibility, without inventing new orchestration.
